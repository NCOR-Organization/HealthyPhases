## **Abstract** 

**Objectives** To analyze the risk factors for developing dysphagia after occipitocervical fusion (OCF) and investigate possible mechanisms and prognosis. 

**Methods** The case data of 43 patients who underwent OCF were retrospectively reviewed. Patients were divided into group A (dysphagia group) and group B (non-dysphagia group) based on Bazaz scoring criteria. Baseline data and imaging parameters were collected: O-C2 angle, C2-7 angle, pharyngeal inlet angle (PIA), posterior occipital cervical angle (POCA), O-EA angle, Oc-Ax angle, Atlas-dens interval, C2-7 sagittal vertical axis (SVA), T1 slope, narrowest oropharyngeal airway space (nPAS), and thickness of the prevertebral soft tissue. Potential risk factors were identified via one-way intergroup comparisons and included in multivariable logistic regression analysis. Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess associations between dnPAS% and each parameter and inter-parameter correlations. Predictors were selected to plot receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for diagnostic evaluation. Prognosis was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and curvilinear regression. 

**Results** Dysphagia occurred in 17 of 43 patients (39.53%). By the final follow-up (≥ 12 months), 11 patients (25.58%) had residual symptoms. Baseline factors, including dyspnea ( _P_ = 0.028), operative segment ( _P_ = 0.021), operative time ( _P_ = 0.006), anesthesia time ( _P_ = 0.025), solitude ( _P_ = 0.019), and satisfaction ( _P_ < 0.001), differed significantly between groups. Imaging parameters dO-C2a ( _P_ < 0.001), dPOCA ( _P_ < 0.001), PoPIA ( _P_ = 0.036), dPIA ( _P_ < 0.001), dOc-Axa ( _P_ = 0.001), dC2-7 SVA ( _P_ = 0.040), PonPAS ( _P_ = 0.004), dnPAS ( _P_ = 0.005), and dnPAS% ( _P_ < 0.001) were also significantly different. Multivariable regression analysis identified dPIA (OR = 0.870, _P_ = 0.008) as an independent protective factor. ROC analysis showed dPIA had good diagnostic value (AUC = 0.855) with a cutoff of -8°. Prognostic analysis indicated rapid recovery was possible by 3 months postoperatively, with full recovery achieved in ~ 30% of patients by 1 year, after which recovery plateaued. 

**Conclusion** Postoperative dysphagia after OCF appears to result from multiple factors involving both “static + dynamic” elements. dPIA is a reliable predictor, with patients having a dPIA >-8° being less likely to develop dysphagia. However, only ~ 30% of patients achieve full recovery.

## **Introduction** 

Occipitocervical fusion (OCF) is a complex and high-risk surgical procedure, primarily due to the intricacies of the craniocervical junction, which contains a variety of anatomical structures and biomechanical mechanisms [1]. In 1994, Goel et al. were the pioneers in reporting the use of atlantoaxial block nailing combined with pedicle screws and plates to stabilize the atlantoaxial spine. Subsequently, in 2001, Harms et al. [2] proposed an enhanced technique that involved the use of universal screws attached to pre-bent titanium rods, leading to the widespread adoption of this approach. Today, this technique is commonly employed in patients experiencing upper cervical instability resulting from rheumatoid diseases, trauma, tumors, tuberculosis, congenital malformations, and other conditions, providing significant advantages in resetting, restoring, and maintaining occipitocervical stability [3]. 

Like any surgical intervention, OCF carries the risk of complications. These may include infection, spinal nerve injury, vertebral artery injury, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, dysphagia, and even respiratory distress. Such complications can significantly impact the clinical outcomes of OCF. Dysphagia, in particular, is often underreported, as patients may not proactively communicate this issue to their healthcare providers. Regrettably, there are relatively few studies addressing postoperative dysphagia following OCF, and many spine surgeons, especially those working in primary care settings, may remain unaware of this complication. Earlier studies indicate that the incidence of postoperative dysphagia after OCF varies between 9.5% and 35.0% [4–7]. The prevailing consensus attributes this condition to secondary stenosis of the pharyngeal cavity due to various factors. However, recent research suggests a multifactorial origin for this complication, involving changes in pharyngeal dynamics mechanism [8, 9] aging and sarcopenia [10, 11] psychological and sociological factors [6, 12, 13] etc. This paper presents a retrospective analysis of the medical records of patients who underwent OCF surgery at our institution, aiming to identify risk factors associated with postoperative dysphagia and to explore the potential mechanisms and prognosis associated with this condition.

## **Methods** 

Medical records of patients who underwent OCF at our hospital from December 2012 to January 2022 were reviewed. The collected data included patients’ gender, age, preoperative diagnosis, surgical method, surgical segment, operation time, anesthesia time, hospitalization duration, and postoperative recovery. Inclusion criteria were as follows: ① Patients must have undergone OCF in our hospital due to disorders such as skull base depression, atlantoaxial fracture, occipitocervical congenital deformity, rheumatoid arthritis, or upper cervical instability due to tumors in the occipitocervical junction area; ② the follow-up period must be ≥ 12 months. Exclusion criteria included: ① missing imaging or relevant case data; ② presence of dysphagia prior to surgery; ③ incomplete follow-up or refusal to participate; ④ a clear diagnosis of postoperative diseases related to the mouth, pharynx, or esophagus. 

A total of 43 cases were included in the study. Of these, 20 patients (46.5%) were male and 23 patients (53.5%) were female. The age range of the patients was 11 to 79 years, with a median age of 59 years. The hospitalization duration varied from 7 to 52 days, with a median of 23 days. Postoperative follow-up ranged from 12 to 125 months, with a median follow-up of 42 months. The diagnoses included 20 cases of atlantoaxial dislocation (46.5%), 18 cases of skull base depression (41.9%), 9 cases with both atlantoaxial dislocation and skull base depression (20.9%), 11 cases of cervical vertebral fracture (25.6%), 6 cases of cervical vertebral tumors (14.0%), and 2 cases of rheumatoid arthritis (4.7%). Additionally, 14 patients (32.6%) presented with cervical spine fusion deformity, 12 cases (27.9%) had cervical disc herniation, 4 cases (9.3%) presented with cervical spinal stenosis, and 4 cases (9.3%) had hypertension; 3 patients (7.0%) had diabetes mellitus, and 3 patients (7.0%) had carotid artery stenosis. The segments operated on included O-C2 (21 cases, 48.8%), O-C3 (11 cases, 25.6%), O-C4 (7 cases, 16.3%), O-C5 (3 cases, 7.0%), and mixed segments (1 case, 2.3%). 

## **Clinical evaluation** 

Dysphagia evaluation: Using the dysphagia scoring system developed by Prof. Bazaz [14], patients were categorized into four grades of eating (Table 1). Follow-up assessments were conducted through outpatient visits or telephone consultations. The timing, severity, and resolution of dysphagia for each patient were documented according to the Bazaz scoring criteria. 

Evaluation of cervical spine function: The Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) Score and the Neck Disability Index (NDI) were used to evaluate cervical function and recovery in patients preoperatively and postoperatively [15]. The JOA score assesses the severity

## **Radiologic parameters** 

Cervical spine radiographs were obtained preoperatively and postoperatively whereas the patients were in a standing position. The following requirements were emphasized: the subject should maintain a neutral gaze at the cervical spine, with both eyes open and shoulders relaxed to prevent obscuring the C7 vertebrae. Standard lateral radiographs were captured in the right lateral position, ensuring the hard palate was parallel to the upper edge of the radiograph, with the angle of the mandible projected appropriately onto the X-ray. The projection was monitored using fluoroscopic television to ensure accuracy, and exposure conditions were set to automatic mode, with X-rays taken by an experienced imaging physician. 

The following lines were defined for measurement purposes: a line drawn from the posterior border of the hard palate to the inferior border of the squamous part of the 

occipital bone (McGregor’s line (O line)), a tangent line between the external occipital protuberance and the foramen magnum (occipital curve line (Oc line)), a tangent line of the inferior endplate of the C2 vertebra (C2 line), and a line connecting the midpoint of the external auditory meatus to the midpoint of the inferior endplate of the C2 vertebra (external acoustic meatus line (EA line)). Additionally, a line representing the posterior margin of the C2 vertebral body (Ax line) was established. Imaging parameters were measured to calculate preoperative (Pre), postoperative (Po), and difference (d) values (Fig. 1). Measurements of the imaging parameters were performed by one attending spine surgeon and two residents, each conducting two repeated measurements within a 2-week interval, with the results averaged. 

- O-C2 angle (O-C2a): the angle formed by McGregor’s line and the C2 line [16, 17]. 

- C2-7 angle (C2-7a): the angle between the parallel lines of the inferior endplates of the C2 and C7 vertebral bodies [18, 19]. 

- Pharyngeal Inlet Angle (PIA): The angle formed by the line connecting the cervical sagittal curvature tangent to the midpoint of the anterior atlantoaxial node and McGregor’s line [5, 6, 9]. 

- O-EA angle (O-EAa): The angle created by McGregor’s line and the EA line [20].

- Oc-Ax angle (Oc-Axa): The angle formed between the Oc line and the Ax line [21]. 

- Narrowest Oropharyngeal Airway Space (nPAS): The narrowest airway measurement from the tip of the uvula to the tip of the epiglottis in the oropharynx. Rate of change is calculated as (dnPAS%=dnPAS/ PrenPAS×100%). [6] 

- Posterior Occipital Cervical Angle (POCA): The angle formed by the Oc line and the line connecting the posterior articular surfaces of the C3 and C4 vertebrae [22]. 

- Atlas-Dens Interval (ADI): The distance between the posterior edge of the atlantoaxial anterior arch and the anterior edge of the odontoid process [5]. 

- C2-7 Sagittal Vertical Axial (C2-7SVA): The distance from the posterior upper corner of C7 to the vertical line that passes through the center of mass of C2 [19]. 

- T1 Slope (T1S): The angle between the tangent line of the superior endplate of the T1 vertebral body and the horizontal line. T1 has been suggested to serve as the “base” of the head and neck; thus, T1S reflects the equilibrium of the cervical vertebrae relative to the total spine. 

- Thickness of the prevertebral soft tissue (T-PVST): The distance from the anterior aspect of the vertebral body to the pharyngeal cavity as observed in imaging, serving as a measure of prevertebral tissue swelling. According to the criteria established by Miyagi et al. [6] prevertebral soft tissue swelling is recognized when the thickness of C2 prevertebral tissue exceeds 6 mm, C3 prevertebral tissue exceeds 7 mm, or C6 prevertebral tissue exceeds 18 mm on lateral cervical radiographs. 

## **Statistical methods** 

Statistical analyses and plotting were conducted using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp, New York, America) and GraphPad Prism 9.4 (GraphPad, USA). Patients were classified into two groups based on the occurrence of postoperative dysphagia. Data were presented as quantitative variables expressed as mean (Mean) ± standard deviation (SD) if they adhered to a normal distribution; otherwise, they were presented as median (M) with interquartile ranges (P25, P75). Qualitative variables were expressed as rates or percentages (%). 

One-way between-group comparisons of clinical baseline data, as well as preoperative and postoperative imaging parameters, were conducted initially. Independent samples t-tests were employed for normally distributed data, whereas nonparametric tests (Mann-Whitney U-test) were utilized for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons of qualitative data, the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was applied. A two-sided _P_ value of less 

than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant, and significant variables were included in the binary multifactorial logistic regression analysis after excluding parameters with significant covariance based on clinical relevance (e.g., surgical/anesthesia time versus surgical segment). Due to the presence of numerous dependent variables and a limited sample size in this study, stepwise forward analysis was implemented to minimize covariance among independent variables and avoid overloading. The model with the highest categorical ability and validity based on the chi-square test was selected. The correlation between dnPAS% and dO-C2a, dPIA, dPOCA, dOc-Axa, and dC2-7 SVA was assessed using linear regression. Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted when both datasets adhered to a normal distribution, whereas Spearman correlation analysis was employed in cases of skewed distribution. 

Potential predictors were identified through both unifactorial and multifactorial analyses. Subject characteristic curves (ROC curves) were plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) and Youden index index were calculated to determine the actual diagnostic value of the potential predictors. 

For prognostic analysis, the association between follow-up duration and postoperative dysphagia was explored using the Kaplan-Meier method, with complete recovery from dysphagia serving as an indicator of achieving a clinical outcome. The regression of dysphagia was analyzed using curvilinear regression. 

## **Results** 

All patients experienced uneventful surgeries with no new spinal cord or nerve injuries, nor any fatalities. A total of 17 patients reported dysphagia at the time of postoperative discharge, resulting in an incidence of 39.53%. Among these, 4 cases (23.53%) were classified as mild, 9 cases (52.94%) as moderate, and 4 cases (23.53%) as severe. At the final follow-up, six patients had fully recovered from dysphagia (one patient underwent revision surgery six months post-operation due to intolerable dysphagia, which was immediately alleviated following the procedure), yielding a recovery rate of 35.29% and a residual rate of 25.58%; the remaining six patients did not experience significant improvement. Notably, five patients in the dysphagia group exhibited postoperative dyspnea, resulting in an incidence of 29.41% within this group, compared to 3.85% in the non-dysphagia group.

## **Prognostic analysis** 

K-M curve analysis indicated that within one year, approximately 30% of patients ultimately recovered their swallowing function without further discomfort, whereas around 70% continued to experience long-term dysphagia (Fig. 6a). Figure 6b illustrates a rapid recovery from dysphagia within the first three months post-surgery, after which the pace and extent of recovery tapered off, with nearly half of the patients sustaining mild dysphagia over an extended period. 

## **Discussion** 

Dysphagia represents a significant complication following OCF. It can manifest at any phase of the swallowing process, with dyspnea appearing as a more severe form of dysphagia following OCF. Currently, there are no standardized criteria for assessing dysphagia resulting from cervical spine surgery. The literature reports two primary categories of assessment methods utilized postoperatively: endoscopic evaluations and questionnaire scoring. The videofluorographic swallowing study (VFSS) serves as the objective benchmark for diagnosing dysphagia and determining its severity [23]. However, in many developing countries, VFSS has not yet gained widespread adoption, leading to a considerable number of patients being unable or unwilling to undergo this examination. Consequently, simpler methods such as the Bazaz score and the swallow quality of life questionnaire (SWAL-QOL) are more commonly employed in clinical practice. Among these, the Bazaz score remains the most widely utilized. However, it has notable drawbacks: (1) its subjectivity; and (2) due to being a four-point scale with limited categories, it lacks the sensitivity to detect subtle differences in swallowing function [24].

## **Occurrence of dysphagia after OCF surgery** 

Dysphagia is not uncommon following OCF and is often overlooked. Previous studies have indicated that the incidence of postoperative dysphagia after OCF ranges from approximately 9.5–35.0% [4–6]. Miyagi et al. [6] found that 7 out of 22 postoperative OCF patients (31.8%) presented with dysphagia; similarly, Wang et al. [5] reported a dysphagia occurrence of 26.6% in one of their singlecenter, retrospective studies. In 2015, Huang et al. [12] first reported dysphagia and dyspnea in two children following OCF. In contrast, our study observed a postoperative dysphagia incidence of 39.5%, decreasing to 25.6% at the final follow-up, which aligns with existing literature. The incidence rate of dyspnea in our cohort was 13.9%, with two patients experiencing acute respiratory obstruction post-surgery. Their conditions stabilized following emergency tracheotomy and tracheal intubation, and they were discharged from the hospital after successful extubation. According to the satisfaction survey conducted as part of this study, the satisfaction score of patients experiencing dysphagia was significantly lower than that of those without ( _P_ < 0.001), indicating that this complication severely impacts the clinical efficacy of OCF. 

## **Mechanisms and factors influencing the occurrence of dysphagia after OCF surgery** 

No clear consensus exists regarding the mechanisms leading to dysphagia, with the predominant view suggesting a association with secondary stenosis of the 

pharyngeal cavity due to various factors, primarily the inability of cervical curvature to match normal physiological function following OCF. Recent years have seen an increasing number of studies pointing to the possibility that this complication may arise in conjunction with multiple contributing factors. 

Secondary stenosis of the pharyngeal cavity: patients who develop postoperative dysphagia after OCF have often been reported to exhibit pharyngeal lumen stenosis on gastrointestinal imaging. Hong et al. [25] reported a case of dysphagia following OCF, characterized by severe narrowing of the pharyngeal lumen confirmed via gastrointestinal angiography. Symptoms improved following revision surgery, with angiography confirming restoration of pharyngeal lumen size. Izeki et al. [17] found a correlation between nPAS reduction and the occurrence and severity of dysphagia. Yoshida et al. [23] suggested that when the occipito-neck is fixed in a flexed position, the mandible is displaced posteriorly, whereas the cardinal vertebrae are shifted anteriorly, resulting in compression of the oropharyngeal space and mechanical narrowing of the pharyngeal cavity. Miyata et al. [16] indicated that dysphagia is likely to occur when the O-C2a angle decreases by more than 5°. Ota et al. [26] conducted a study involving healthy volunteers, demonstrating that for every 10° reduction in the O-C2a angle, there is a corresponding 37% decrease in the normalized pharyngeal airway space (nPAS). Similarly, Izeki et al. [17] performed a follow-up analysis of 13 postoperative patients who underwent OCF and found that each 10°

Other factors: recently, it has been suggested that secondary pharyngeal stenosis may be just one localized factor contributing to dysphagia, whereas several potential confounding factors are frequently overlooked. 

Altered swallowing kinetics: the introduction of VFSS technology has enhanced clinicians’ ability to visualize dysphagia. In their study, Ebata et al. [8] assessed seven postoperative OCF patients through VFSS and a multidisciplinary evaluation. Of these patients, five exhibited imaging abnormalities related to swallowing; however, only two reported subjective symptoms, and none demonstrated significant structural stenosis. In our current study, we observed that four out of twenty-six patients in the normal group presented with significantly abnormal imaging parameters, whereas two out of seventeen patients in the dysphagia group had imaging results within the normal range. Kaneyama et al. [9] addressed this apparent “paradox” by noting that the oropharyngeal cavity is a dynamic structure. Previous static evaluations of imaging parameters have hindered the identification of conclusive evidence regarding the pathogenesis of dysphagia. Specifically, posterior displacement of the root of the tongue or the mandible observed in static radiographs does not necessarily indicate pharyngeal 

stenosis, as the remarkable flexibility of the tongue allows for spatial adjustments within the pharyngeal cavity. Consequently, we assert that, when comparing imaging parameters against static factors, the dynamics of the pharynx must not be overlooked. “Static + dynamic” factors play a crucial role in understanding the development, progression, and regression of this complication, highlighting the significance of their interplay. 

Aging and sarcopenia: with the accelerating trend of an aging society, an increasing number of elderly patients are undergoing OCF surgery. Miura et al. [11] reported a case involving an 85-year-old male patient who had OCF performed due to high spinal cord disease. Unfortunately, this patient developed severe dysphagia in the immediate postoperative period. Despite the surgical team maintaining the parameters of O-C2a, S line, C2-C7a within the ideal range during the operation, the VFSS indicated significant obstruction due to a large volume of viscous fluid in the larynx. This finding suggests that the elderly patient may have experienced postoperative dysphagia caused by “sarcopenic dysphagia,” a condition attributed to the weakness of swallowing muscles. The Japanese Society for Rehabilitation of Dysphagia [10] defines “sarcopenic dysphagia” as dysphagia resulting from a reduction in both generalized and swallowing-related muscle tissue. This study also identified a 75-year-old male patient who exhibited a 1-mm increase in postoperative nPAS, alongside a BMI of 17.0, which fell to 16.5 at the last follow-up (two years post-surgery). This patient continued to experience residual moderate dysphagia, with imaging showing no significant narrowing of the pharyngeal cavity. It is proposed that a vicious cycle may exist between sarcopenia and dysphagia, hindering the recovery of postoperative patients. However, the underlying pathomechanisms remain unclear. It is hypothesized that the muscle tissue responsible for swallowing functions originates from a different embryonic lineage compared to somatic muscle, rendering it more vulnerable to dystrophy and atrophy due to the constant demands of respiratory and digestive motions. 

Psychological and sociological factors: Some studies suggest that prolonged postoperative dysphagia should be regarded as both a “physical and mental illness”. A team of Japanese rehabilitation physicians [6] argues that psychiatric factors may exacerbate pharyngeal trauma, whether from tracheal intubation or other surgical interventions, after surgery. Patients appear to be particularly sensitive to this “invisible trauma,” and this potential risk factor might contribute to some instances of unexplained postoperative dysphagia. Additionally, we observed that all four elderly individuals living alone experienced dysphagia, which highlights the importance for clinicians to address both physical and psychological aspects to avoid the therapeutic challenges posed by corrective imaging.

## **Regression of postoperative dysphagia in OCF** 

The regression of dysphagia following this complication has been rarely reported. Tatter et al. [7] observed onethird of patients experienced resolution of symptoms, which resulted in a long-term post-OCF dysphagia incidence of 16.5%. Sakai et al. [24] observed in a postoperative follow-up of patients who underwent posterior cervical spine fusion that 23.8% continued to experience dysphagia at 6 months post-surgery. This percentage remained relatively unchanged at the 1-year mark, potentially confirming the long-term persistence of O-C2a dysphagia due to immobilization from bony fusion, as stated by Izeki et al. [17] They note that fixation of O-C2a from osseous fusion complicates spontaneous recovery of dysphagia, leading to long-lasting difficulties. Our prognostic analysis suggests that the rapid recovery phase for dysphagia occurs within the first 3 months post-surgery; thereafter, the rate of recovery diminishes significantly. Approximately 30% of patients achieve normal swallowing function within 1 year post-operation, but beyond this period, most patients struggle with spontaneous recovery, experiencing lasting mild dysphagia (with a recovery rate noted at the last follow-up of only 35.29%). We propose that dysphagia resulting from occipitocervical hyperflexion may be long-lasting and challenging to resolve without modifications to the upper cervical curvature. Conversely, dysphagia resulting from non-cervical curvature may benefit from non-surgical treatment approaches aimed at alleviating symptoms. However, overall, the outlook for recovery from this complication remains grim; dysphagia often is not the primary complaint for many patients, which can delay their access to necessary treatment and rehabilitation. 

## **Limitations** 

This study is retrospective, conducted at a single center, and features a relatively small sample size alongside numerous variables. Moreover, dysphagia is assessed subjectively, lacking robust objective evaluation metrics; the Bazaz scale is limited to a four-point system, which fails to capture subtle changes in dysphagia. The followup period coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, raising the possibility that the symptoms in some patients may have been influenced by the novel coronavirus. Additionally, whereas PIA offers good diagnostic value and is straightforward to measure, measurement errors may increase in cases of atlanto-occipital deformity and subluxation. Thus, there is no definitive, reliable imaging “gold standard” to determine the occurrence of postoperative dysphagia. We anticipate that future statistical analyses of larger datasets will enhance our understanding of the mechanisms and prognoses related to this complication. 

## **Conclusion** 

Despite these limitations, postoperative dysphagia following OCF can be viewed as a multifactorial complication, characterized by both “static + dynamic” factors. The prognosis remains challenging and warrants careful attention from clinicians.
