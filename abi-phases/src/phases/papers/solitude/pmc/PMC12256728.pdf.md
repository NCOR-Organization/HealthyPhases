## **Abstract** 

Being alone is a basic and prevalent human experience, yet little is known about its effects on social identity. When alone, social identity may weaken because individuals feel their groups are less valuable or less salient. Conversely, it may strengthen because craving for bonding increases ingroup perceived value. We tested these ideas with five experiments ( _N_ = 1312). Study 1, using existing groups, and Study 2, using minimal groups, showed that aloneness (vs. being with others) reduced in- group identity through its effect on in- group esteem. In Study 3, this effect was equivalent to the effect observed following social exclusion. Study 4 showed that individuals alone were indifferent in allocating money between in- group and out- group members. Last, Study 5 indicated that these effects do not stem from reduced salience of in- group members when alone. In conclusion, an alone mindset affects social identity by decreasing in- group esteem, signalling broad social implications for this basic social condition.

## **BACKGROUND** 

It is a human condition, often a basic need, to be a part of social entities (Baumeister & Leary, 1995). Individuals seek to be part of groups and often construe their social reality in terms of social categories (Fiske & Neuberg, 1990; Tajfel et al., 1971; Turner et al., 1994). Our ability to consider and join others allowed us to develop advanced cooperation and a cultural way of living, improving our well- being and chances of survival (Baumeister, 2005). In fact, the reality is that individuals are often part of social groups but are physically alone (Guerin, 2001; Spears, 1995). The **— —** impact of this physical condition that is, aloneness on group- related attitudes and behaviours is a topic that has received scant research attention thus far and was the focus of the present investigation. 

Aloneness (sometimes referred to as solitude) is an important, yet understudied, state of human existence. Aloneness can be defined as a time when one is away from and not interacting with other people (Uziel & Schmidt- Barad, 2022). Research indicates that the amount of time people spend alone is increasing, exceeding 50% for some individuals, varying by factors such as age, familial status, and occupational status (Anttila et al., 2020; Atalay, 2024; Mehl & Pennebaker, 2003). By and large, research on aloneness has focused on inward- directed emotional experiences resulting from this situation. Studies documented both negative and positive experiences. Winnicott (1958) considered being alone a situation that demands a capacity developed in early childhood to manage well. Often, aloneness is experienced as a time of passivity and loneliness (Larson, 1990), and was found to be associated with lower positive affect and higher biological indices of stress (Pauly et al., 2017). Relatedly, Wilson et al. (2014) demonstrated how aversive people may find even brief periods of aloneness, and research showed that aloneness predicted low motivation and lack of self- control (mainly among high- neuroticism individuals; Uziel, 2016). 

Notwithstanding, recent studies have documented positive alone experiences. For example, in Uziel (2021) individuals described their experiences alone as more relaxed and less negative than their time with others, and as a period that allows them to develop their interests. Similarly, aloneness was found to be a period of emotion regulation and relative calmness (Nguyen et al., 2018). Participants in Weinstein et al. (2021) emphasized a sense of autonomy and self- connection during their time alone, and aloneness was even associated with a stronger flow in one's activities (Liu & Csikszentmihalyi, 2020). 

Clearly, the impact of aloneness on emotional experiences takes many forms (Lay et al., 2019; Long et al., 2003), implying that this condition is richer and more dynamic than previously considered. While there has been notable progress in understanding the emotional aspects of being alone, few studies have broadened the scope to examine how aloneness influences social attitudes, and, more specifically, social identity, defined as a sense of belonging to groups (Ellemers et al., 1999). 

According to social identity theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979) and self- categorization theory (Turner et al., 1994), individuals constantly categorize themselves in terms of group affiliation and apply this identity to differentiate themselves from others. Social identities supplement individuals' personal identities and can co- exist alongside them. Much of the research on group identities was directed to explain group phenomena, such as cohesiveness, competition and stereotyping (e.g., Hunter et al., 2017; Jaubert et al., 2022). Of special interest in the present context is the flexibility of social identities. According to Turner et al. (1994, see also Hogg & Turner, 1987), social identities are flexible and context- dependent, such that changes in the salience of situational cues affect their content. However, research on the effect of basic social situations (e.g., aloneness) on social identity is scant. 

Some research was conducted in the context of loneliness—defined as distress caused by feeling socially isolated (Cacioppo et al., 2015)—indicating that it loosens social bonds. Studies show that loneliness predicts self- centeredness, expressed in reduced attention to others and their needs (Cacioppo et al., 2017) and that it predicted a preference for a larger interpersonal distance within an interpersonal space, reflecting alienation from others (Layden et al., 2018; Lieberz et al., 2021). Relatedly, in a largescale study, lonely individuals expected others to reject them and perceived others as less trustworthy, thwarting their hope to build social bonds (Bellucci, 2020). It thus appears that when individuals feel lonely, they have less faith in others, and consequently further distance themselves from them, implying that their social identity weakens. Supporting this line of thought is a substantial body of research showing that social identities help in curbing loneliness and improving well- being (Bonetto et al., 2022; Evans et al., 2022; Haslam et al., 2022; van Dick et al., 2023). 

That said, findings about loneliness do not always apply to aloneness. Loneliness is a feeling, whereas aloneness is an objective state. When people are alone, they are likely to feel lonely (Larson, 1990), but people can (and often do) feel lonely while being with other people (Katz et al., 2024). In addition,

whereas loneliness is an intense negative feeling, aloneness is not necessarily negative, but, as noted above, is often quite positive (Lay et al., 2019). Thus, loneliness only informs about potential processes occurring alone. 

Another basis for predictions about the impact of aloneness on social identity comes from theoretical work on self- categorization. As noted above, according to the self- categorization theory, individuals hold a dual perspective of themselves as private entities alongside their existence as part of a group (Turner et al., 1994). Self- perception often varies between these two perspectives and the emphasis shifts by the salience of psychological and objective factors associated with each perspective (Turner & Oakes, 1989). Given that the salience of external entities is reduced alone, one can expect a stronger presence of the private self over the public (i.e., group- based) identity in one's cognition (Carver & Scheier, 1981). A few studies have documented effects consistent with this prediction. For example, Uziel (2021) conducted a text analysis of individuals' experiences when alone versus when they are with other people. Its results showed that social motives (e.g., need to belong) were less salient alone compared to when participants were with others, implying that social identities may be downplayed alone. Studies showing that a stronger need to belong enhances group identity are also in line with this idea (Theodorakis et al., 2012). 

However, the literature also offers an alternative prediction, pointing to the possibility that aloneness may increase social identity. Tomova et al. (2020) showed that a period of aloneness increased the craving for social interaction, akin to a craving for food after fasting. This implies that one may find more attractive and thus think more highly of the social entities that define one's social identity when alone (see also Saporta et al., 2021). 

A different approach leading to a similar prediction was suggested by studies on the effects of deindividuation on adherence to group norms, as suggested in the social identity model of deindividuation (Reicher et al., 1995; Spears, 1995). In some of these studies, de- individuation was manipulated by asking participants to be alone (vs. with in- group members) during the study (Spears et al., 1990). It was suggested that being part of a group but physically separate from its members paradoxically increases adherence to group norms because in this state visual intra- group differences are minimized, leading to a perception that inter- group differences are more substantial (Reicher, 1984). However, the results of three studies yielded equivocal results. De- individuation, manipulated via aloneness, increased adherence to group norms in Spears et al. (1990), but only partially (i.e., for a subsample and only some of the measures) in Reicher (1984). A study by Reicher et al. (1998), which measured group identification, is especially relevant in the present context. In this study, participants were either in a joint session of ingroup members or worked alone. Its results (Studies 1 and 3) showed that aloneness had no significant effect on group identity, presumably because identity was sufficiently established among the participants. However, in contrast, Study 2 in Reicher et al. (1998), applying a very similar procedure to Studies 1 and 3, yielded a significantly higher identification in the alone group. 

Taken together, the impact of aloneness on group identity is an open question, addressed by a relatively small number of studies, which yielded inconsistent findings. Theoretical accounts vary widely covering a wide range of effects. Given the limited empirical evidence on this question and the increasing role that aloneness has in our culture, there is interest in increasing understanding of this issue. 

## **The present research** 

In the present research, we aimed to investigate the effects of aloneness on social identity in a series of 5 experiments. Given the contrasting predictions in the extant literature, we approached this question with an open mind and sought to test it in diverse settings using existing and minimal groups. Our focus was on the immediate effect of aloneness on the sense of group worth (i.e., group esteem). As noted, arguments exist that when alone, individuals feel their groups are less valuable to an extent that makes them feel alienated (Lieberz et al., 2021). An opposing idea was that aloneness creates a craving for one's group, implying an increase in its perceived value (Tomova et al., 2020).

We tested these ideas in Studies 1–3 by looking at how aloneness—specifically, an alone mindset—affects group esteem. Group esteem is defined as the positive or negative value associated with belonging to a specific group (Ellemers et al., 1999). Although we did not have an a- priori directional prediction for how aloneness would affect group esteem, given the existing theories, we expected changes in group esteem to mediate the effects of aloneness on group identity. That is, to the extent that aloneness affects group identity, it should be because it affects how valuable individuals perceive their groups to be. 

Study 1 aimed to investigate this question in real- life existing groups. In Study 2 we tested this question in newly formed minimal groups. In Study 3, we added inclusion and exclusion conditions over the alone and ‘with others’ conditions, thereby gaining contextual insights into the psychological experience of aloneness. In Study 4, we explored behavioural implications by looking at monetary decisions in an inter- group context. Lastly, as noted above, some approaches suggest that while alone the salience of one's in- group decreases (Turner & Oakes, 1989) and that this mechanism could explain a reduction in group identity. Therefore, in Study 5, we investigated the salience of in- group (vs. out- group) in an alone (vs. with others) condition. Jointly, the studies document and explain the impact of an alone mindset on group processes. 

Because we sought to focus on group cognitions, and because trait self- esteem was found to correlate with these cognitions (Hunter et al., 2017), we measured and controlled for trait self- esteem. Controlling for trait self- esteem did not change any of the conclusions in the Studies and thus is not reported in detail in the main text. However, the Data S1 file presents all the analyses with this control in place. 

Lastly, as noted, we use mindset manipulation to induce aloneness, and we do so for practical and substantive reasons. Mindset manipulations are common in research on (mere) social presence and its control condition (and the present focus)—aloneness. They take many forms, such as guided imagination (e.g., Meyer et al., 2019; Noah et al., 2018; Uziel, 2016), placing an image of open/closed eyes or faces on the test page (e.g., Reis & Pfister, 2022), using avatars (e.g., Shteynberg, 2010; Sidarus et al., 2020), informing participants that they are about to enter (or not) a social interaction (e.g., Erber et al., 1996), that others may (or may not) read the content they create (e.g., Jacob et al., 2021), or, retroactively, that others observed them (vs. not; e.g., Steinmetz et al., 2016). The findings from these studies yield effects on par with effects based on the physical state of social presence/aloneness. 

Our use of a mindset procedure thus followed existing research practices, but it has also sought to address problems inherent in the experimental research on aloneness. A central reason for the relative paucity of experimental work on aloneness stems from a gap between lab- based and real- life aloneness. Research shows that participants do not feel alone in a laboratory room (for a good reason, as they are often monitored; e.g., Bond & Titus, 1983; Uziel, 2007). Importantly, even when they are truly alone, individuals do not experience the same level of privacy typical of being alone in their natural environment (Griffin, 2001; Noah et al., 2018; Uziel, 2007). The alone mindset manipulation was thus devised as a more ecologically valid approximation of the real- life alone condition than what laboratory- based manipulations of aloneness often achieve (Uziel, 2016; Uziel & Hefetz, 2014). 

Evidence from previous applications of the current alone mindset indicates that it differs from loneliness and that it creates an experience similar to a real- life alone experience. Quantitative analysis of the content created by individuals following the alone mindset manipulation ( _N_ = 1717; Uziel, 2021), revealed that it was not marked by the negatively laden content typical of loneliness (i.e., distress and longing for social contact; Cacioppo et al., 2015), but by a more nuanced content, echoing experiences one finds in real- life aloneness, such as greater control of time, liberty in pursuing leisure activities, and a fair share of positive feelings and some level of sadness. Importantly, two recent studies have shown that mindset manipulations yield effects equivalent to real- life aloneness. In Uziel et al. (2020), the level of trust in others that (high- neuroticism) participants expressed using the current mindset manipulation (Study 3) was equivalent to results in an experience- sampling procedure with real- life aloneness (Study 4). In Uziel and Schmidt- Barad (2022), thinking about themselves alone/with others (Study 1) was associated with equivalent effects to actually being in these situations in a 10- day experience sampling study (Study 2) focused on affect and well- being.

## **Power analysis and open science declaration** 

Building on previous studies with a similar design, we expected a small- medium effect size for the 2 aloneness mindset manipulation (2 _r_ = .20, _η_ p ~ .04; Uziel, 2016), alongside a relatively strong effect size ( _r_ = .50; _η_ p ~ .25; Hunter et al., 2017) for the in- group esteem- in- group identity association. In Studies 1–3 we tested these relations using mediation models. A- priori power analyses (using ‘MedPower’; Kenny, 2017) revealed that 193 participants secured an 80% power for detecting indirect (i.e., mediated) effects with these anchors. Our sample sizes exceeded 200 participants (208 ≤ _N_ ≤ 326), which also secured 80% power in Studies 4 and 5 (which tested direct effects; Faul et al., 2009). Materials, data, and code for analysis of all the studies are stored in the Open Science Framework: https:// osf. io/ gp2tn/ . 

## **STUDY 1** 

## **Overview** 

Study 1 tested the effect of aloneness on social identity relating to existing, real- life, groups. Specifically, we tested whether British group identity weakens (vs. is enhanced) because of changes in group esteem in an Alone mindset versus a With Others mindset. 

## **Method** 

## Participants and procedure 

Participants ( _N_ = 208) were recruited through the online platform Prolific (www. proli fic. com) for a study about attitudes. Participants were pre- screened to include only UK residents and were also asked about their nationality in the survey. One participant who was not British was excluded. Three participants who failed attention checks were also excluded, setting the final sample at _N_ = 204 (102 Male, 101 Female, 1 Non- binary; _M_ age = 37.97 years, SD = 13.82 years). All participants were British since birth (except for 4 participants, who were British for +15 years) and native English speakers. 

Participants started the study by completing a _trait self- esteem_ scale. They were then randomly assigned to an _Alone_ or a _With Others_ mindset manipulation. On completion, participants completed a measure of _group esteem_ followed by _group identity_ , both relating to their British identity. Participants then completed a demographic questionnaire, were thanked, and debriefed. 

## Materials 

## _Trait self- esteem_ 

Self- esteem was measured with the 10- item Rosenberg self- esteem scale (Rosenberg, 1965). Participants rated items such as ‘on the whole, I am satisfied with myself’ along a _strongly disagree_ (1) to _strongly agree_ (4) scale ( _α_ = .93). 

## _Alone/with others social context mindset manipulation_ 

Participants were randomly primed to adopt either an _Alone_ ( _n_ = 103) or a _With Others_ ( _n_ = 101) social context mindset by completing six identical sentences with a single word or a short sentence: ‘when I am by myself, I…’ (Alone) or ‘when I am in the company of others, I…’ (With Others). Participants had to provide a different answer to each sentence. Past research has documented the validity of the task and the similarities in the effects it produces to real- life alone and ‘with others’ conditions (Uziel, 2016, 2021; Uziel et al., 2020).

## _Group esteem_ 

We measured group esteem with a 4- item scale, adapted from Hunter et al. (2017) to refer to British identity. Participants rated items such as ‘I feel good about being British’ along a _strongly disagree_ (1) to _strongly agree_ (7) scale ( _α_ = .90). 

## _Group identity_ 

We measured the strength of group identification with a 3- item scale, adapted from Hunter et al. (2017) to refer to British identity. Participants rated items such as ‘I often think about the fact that I am British’ along a _strongly disagree_ (1) to _strongly agree_ (7) scale ( _α_ = .89). 

## **Results** 

We started by checking the effectiveness of our manipulation. Two judges blind to condition assignment judged the responses provided on the Alone/With Others mindset task and rated whether they reflected an alone experience or a ‘with others’ experience along a _definitely alone_ (1) to _definitely with others_ (5) scale. The judges were in strong agreement (ICC = .95), and their ratings were averaged. Results showed a strong effect, indicating that participants in the Alone condition ( _M_ = 1.16, SD = 0.29) described experiences judged to reflect being alone, and participants in the With Others condition ( _M_ = 4.62, SD = 0.73) described experiences judged to reflect being with other people, _t_ (202) = −44.70, _p_ < .001, Cohen's _d_ = −6.26. 

Descriptive statistics across the conditions of Study 1 are presented in Table 1, which shows that group identity and esteem were strongly positively related, and that trait self- esteem was positively associated with group esteem. 

We proceeded to the main analysis, testing the effect of an Alone (vs. With Others) mindset on group identity with group esteem as a mediator. We tested this effect using a mediation model with 5000 bootstrapped samples to estimate the confidence intervals. Results are summarized in Table 2 and illustrated in Figure 1. 

Results showed that there was no direct effect of the alone/with others condition on in- group identity. However, importantly, and crucially to test mediation (Hayes, 2009; Igartua & Hayes, 2021; Zhao et al., 2010), there was a significant indirect (i.e., mediated) effect, _p_ = .012. The mediated effect indicates that compared with the With Others mindset (coded 1), an Alone mindset (coded 0) reduced in- group esteem and through that reduced in- group identity. That is, individuals thinking about themselves alone found their in- group less appealing, less valuable, and thus felt less attached to it.[1]

## **STUDY 2** 

Study 2 sought to expand Study 1 by exploring the effect of an Alone mindset on group identity through its effect on group esteem using a minimal group design. By this, we tried to extend our findings and make a more general statement about the effect of basic social conditions on group processes (Otten, 2016). 

## **Method** 

## Participants and procedure 

Participants ( _N_ = 240) were recruited through Prolific for a study about attitudes. Of the initial sample, eleven participants were excluded from analyses because they failed attention checks ( _n_ = 5) or did not know their group affiliation ( _n_ = 6), setting the final sample at _N_ = 229 (117 Male, 110 Female, 2 Non- binary; _M_ age = 40.76 years, SD = 13.12 years). All participants were native English speakers, and all (except for four participants) were British.

Participants started by completing a _trait self- esteem_ scale. On completion, they worked on tasks that served to form minimal groups. To this end, they performed an ‘Aesthetic Preference Test’, which ostensibly served to assign them into two groups (Group A or B) of like- minded people. In practice, all were part of Group A, thinking that some other participants were assigned to Group B. Next was a task intended to enhance the group assignment manipulation. Participants worked on a _‘spot the difference’ task_ and were told that the performance of all members of each group was combined and that five participants of the winning group would be eligible for a bonus. Once completed, participants were randomly assigned to an _Alone_ or a _With Others_ mindset manipulation. Next, participants completed measures of _group esteem_ followed by _group identity_ , both relating to their newly formed Group A. Participants then completed a demographic questionnaire, were thanked, and debriefed. 

## Materials 

## _Trait self- esteem_ 

Like in Study 1, trait self- esteem was measured with the Rosenberg self- esteem scale (Rosenberg, 1965; _α_ = .91). 

## _Group formation_ 

Group formation had two parts. The first was based on an ‘Aesthetic Preference Test’ (Petersen & Blank, 2003). Participants were presented with 5 famous paintings (e.g., the Mona Lisa), and were instructed to rank them by personal preference. After their rankings (and a short wait for ‘data processing’) participants were notified that they were divided into two groups of like- minded individuals (Group A or Group B). In practice, all participants were notified that they were in Group A. 

Participants then proceeded to a second task framed as a group activity. They were asked to solve a ‘spot the difference’ puzzle (find 10 differences between two drawings) and told that the performance of all members of each group would be aggregated and that 5 (random) participants of the winning group would be eligible for a bonus prize (because all participants were in the same group, we choose 5 winners in a raffle to receive the bonus). We verified that participants knew their group affiliation with a question at the end of the survey and excluded (six) participants who failed. 

## _Alone/with others social context mindset manipulation_ 

Like in Study 1, participants were randomly primed to adopt either an _Alone_ ( _n_ = 111) or a _With Others_ ( _n_ = 118) social context mindset with the sentence completion task. 

## _Group esteem_ 

We measured group esteem with the 4- item group self- esteem subscale of the scale by Ellemers et al. (1999). Participants were asked to address their group (Group A) and rate items such as ‘I feel good about my group’ along a _strongly disagree_ (1) to _strongly agree_ (7) scale ( _α_ = .77). 

## _Group identity_ 

We measured the strength of group identification with the 4- item group- centrality subscale (Cameron, 2004), which includes items such as: ‘I think about being a part of my group’, ‘The fact that I am a member of my group doesn't really enter my mind (Reversed)’. Participants were asked to address their group (Group A) and rate items along a _strongly disagree_ (1) to _strongly agree_ (7) scale. The reliability of the scale was good ( _α_ = .82).

We started by checking the effectiveness of our manipulation with the same judgement procedure from Study 1. The two judges were in strong agreement (ICC = .96), and their ratings were averaged. ## **STUDY 3** 

Study 3 introduced a modification and an addition to the previous studies. First, we wanted to generalize our manipulation. To this end, we used a different approach whereby participants were asked to imagine themselves and write elaborately about their experiences alone or with others (vs. writing their associations with a word or a short sentence). Considering the findings of Studies 1 and 2, there was reason to expect that the Alone mindset would induce attitudes closer to exclusion than to inclusion, and vice- versa for the With Others mindset.

## **STUDY 4** 

Studies 1–3 established that mere thought about oneself when alone induced notable changes in group esteem, and subsequently in group identity. In an Alone mindset, participants experienced their (existing or minimal) groups as less valuable. We sought to explore a potential implication of this shift by studying behavioural resource allocation. Specifically, participants were assigned to (minimal) groups, experienced (Alone/With Others) mindset manipulation, and were then asked to allocate resources to other members from either their in- group or out- group. We expected that individuals in the Alone mindset would not show the same in- group favouritism as participants in the With Others mindset (Hunter et al., 2017).

Participants were randomly assigned to allocate money to their in- group (self- excluded) or out- group members. Upon completion, they filled out a demographic questionnaire, were thanked, and debriefed.

## **Discussion** 

Thinking about themselves with other people has led participants to award relatively large sums of money to their in- group members, and relatively small sums to members of an out- group. Such effects, reflecting in- group favouritism (Hunter et al., 2017), were eliminated by the mere thought of oneself when alone. Thinking about their time alone made participants indifferent to their group affiliation. Adding on the results of Studies 1–3, the present findings show that an Alone mindset may affect decision- making and behaviours with tangible implications for groups. However, could the alone mindset simply reduce the salience and attention to in- group members and by that reduce group identity? We sought to check this more directly in Study 5. 

## **STUDY 5** 

Studies 1–3 showed that an Alone mindset weakens group identity through changes in group esteem. Study 4 showed that the reduction in the value of one's in- group translated into tangible actions. These studies imply that aloneness affects group identity through a motivational path. When alone, individuals may turn indifferent toward their in- group, value it less, and be less attached to it, as is often implied in conditions of loneliness (Cacioppo et al., 2017; Uziel et al., 2020). In our final study, we sought to test whether these effects can be explained by (or reduced to) attentional processes. That is, to test whether individuals alone are less attentive to their in- group members (Turner & Oakes, 1989). To test for this possibility, the present study tested participants' recollection of details related to their group members (Brewer et al., 1995) following the Alone/With Others manipulation. 

## **Method** 

## Participants and procedure 

We recruited _N_ = 237 participants through Prolific for a study about personality and attitudes. Of the initial sample, six participants were excluded from analyses because they failed attention checks ( _n_ = 2), did not know their group affiliation ( _n_ = 1), or completed the survey on their mobile phone ( _n_ = 3) against an instruction not to do so because the stimuli materials could not fit small screens, setting the final sample at _N_ = 231 (115 Male, 116 Female; _M_ age = 42.20 years, SD = 13.04 years). For the _recall_ , participants were asked 12 true/false questions on the details they had read about the other participants. ## **Discussion** 

Study 5 sought to test whether the Alone (vs. With Others) mindset affects the attention allocated to in- group versus out- group members. To this end, participants were exposed to information from both groups and were later tested on their recall. As expected, the recall was better for in- group information, indicating that participants were more attentive to their in- group members (Brewer et al., 1995). However, the mindset condition (Alone/With Others) did not moderate this effect. It therefore appears that aloneness does not make one less attentive to group information. Thoughts about in- group members occupy the alone mind, and the difference appears to be in the value attached to these thoughts, which take a negative turn. 

## **GENER AL DISCUSSION** 

Aloneness is on the rise. More people choose singlehood as their way of living (van den Berg & Verbakel, 2022), and more people find themselves alone regardless of their chosen way of life (Atalay, 2024). This reality may be responsible to some extent for the rise in loneliness (Bellucci, 2020), has substantial implications for personal well- being (Uziel & Schmidt- Barad, 2022), and could affect societal cohesiveness. The present investigation sought to address aspects relating to the intersection of personal and societal lives by studying how aloneness (specifically, an alone mindset) affects social identity. The extant literature on this topic is quite limited and offers contrasting predictions, which we sought to disentangle and test in a series of experiments. 

Study 1 addressed participants' existing social affiliations. British participants were primed with an Alone or a With Others mindset and reported about their in- group esteem and identity. Results showed that aloneness reduced in- group esteem, which then mediated the effect on in- group identity. The mere thought of being alone was sufficient to reduce the value of established group affiliation, and subsequently, the level of identification with it. Study 2 tested this hypothesis using a minimal group paradigm, thereby controlling for the effects of prior group history. The results were similar. Again,

aloneness decreased in- group esteem, which mediated a reduction in in- group identity. Seeking to understand the impact of aloneness on social identity more deeply, Study 3 introduced two additional conditions: social inclusion and social exclusion. Its findings revealed that aloneness yielded a similar mediated reduction in in- group esteem to the reduction observed following social exclusion. Study 4 showed that the effect of aloneness can materialize into actions and affect behaviours. When alone, participants were indifferent in allocating (real) money between their in- group and out- group members. Study 5 showed that these effects do not stem from reduced attention to in- group members in the Alone condition. Jointly, the findings show that aloneness carries substantial effects on group- related attitudes and behaviour. By the mere thought of themselves alone, individuals consistently felt that their in- groups were less valuable and were expressing less attachment to them. 

These findings carry several theoretical implications. Predictions based on the extant literature about the impact of aloneness on group identity varied considerably and suggested that both directions of impact were viable. Specifically, by some accounts, aloneness could decrease identity because individuals may, to some extent, resent their group and consider it less valuable (Uziel et al., 2020), or because group- related thoughts are less salient when alone (Turner & Oakes, 1989). An alternative account was that aloneness induces a craving that raises the perceived value of one's in- group (Tomova et al., 2020) and that being apart from other group members decreases perceived differences, thereby increasing identity (Reicher et al., 1998). Our findings lend support to the former ideas (i.e., reduction in group esteem while being alone), and specifically to the suggestions that aloneness may increase distancing (Larson, 1990; Uziel et al., 2020). We found consistent and notable reductions in group esteem and behaviours associated with alienation toward one's in- group when alone. Our Study 5 indicated that issues related to the salience of group members' attributes do not account for these effects. 

The present findings further expand the current literature on aloneness by documenting psychological implications resulting from this condition that have not been considered thus far. Recent writings have shown the complex emotional expressions associated with aloneness (Lay et al., 2019; Nguyen et al., 2018; Pauly et al., 2017), yet little was known about other psychological responses. The present research shows that aloneness affects attitudes, that these attitudes may involve external agents, and that they may translate into social behaviours with potentially broad social implications. In addition, the findings also extend the literature on loneliness. Individuals alone are prone to feel lonely (Larson, 1990), and loneliness is associated with social alienation (Bellucci, 2020). However, aloneness is distinct from loneliness, and the present findings (notably, Study 3) demonstrated this in showing that the alone experience was not as negative as social exclusion (the dominant experience in loneliness). Despite that, the effects of aloneness in terms of reduction in group esteem were as strong as those of social exclusion, indicating that implications of loneliness may find root in objective daily social conditions, and can thus be potentially mitigated by addressing them. 

There are several limitations to the present research. First, the study was focused on Alone and With Others mindsets. Past research has shown that the mindset priming manipulation yields similar effects to the actual states (e.g., Uziel et al., 2020) and that priming aloneness can achieve a more valid reflection of reality than lab- based manipulations of aloneness (because participants do not believe to be or feel truly alone in the lab; Noah et al., 2018). Notwithstanding, testing these effects in the context of the actual physical states could add valuable information. Second, relatedly, the effects studied were brief, and the current data cannot attest to their duration or persistence. Additional studies are needed addressing the temporal dynamics of group- related attitudes in this realm. Third, the present research did not investigate individual differences in the alone experience (beyond trait self- esteem). There are differences between individuals in preference for solitude (Burger, 1995), in the ability to cope with aloneness (Uziel, 2016), and in the need to belong (Leary et al., 2013), which could moderate the effects, and should be pursued further. Fourth, the present research was focused on attitudes toward in- groups. However, the effects could extend to inter- group relations, and this topic deserves more attention in the future.
